Features: time back in your day

Ordered by how much of your day I expect each to touch. The first two remove steps you repeat every working day.

F1

Calendar sync adds new meetings by itself

My pickS–M

When .agenda.json gains a meeting for the open day, its section appears in time order without the review dialog. The review stays for anything that removes or moves a section with content.

Why: today only an empty note fills itself. Any later change lights the dot and costs Ctrl+Shift+C, a dialog and a confirm, several times a day, for a change that cannot lose anything. A setting keeps the current behaviour available.

F2

Copy a meeting as minutes

My pickWas C2, "maybe later"M

One command on a section puts ready-to-paste minutes on the clipboard, as rich text for Outlook and Teams and as plain text: title and date, decisions (the => lines), actions with owner and due date, topics left open. In the UI language.

Why: writing the follow-up mail is retyping what the note already holds. It was parked in October as too early; since then the tokenizer, the export formats and the list clipboard were built, so this is now mostly composition of existing parts.

F3

Suggestions while typing @names, (topics) and section titles

My pickM

After @, after ( behind an action symbol, and on a line being made a section title, a small list offers what your notes already use, most recent first. Tab or Enter takes one; typing on ignores it.

Why: the app has no completion at all. Everything downstream matches exact text: has:@dana, tag:, and Section History, which loses an occurrence when a title differs by one character. Suggestions prevent the variants instead of repairing them.

F4

Automatic backup outside the notes folder

My pickS–M

Once a week (and on demand) ChronoNote writes one bundle of all notes to a folder of your choice that OneDrive does not sync, and keeps the last eight.

Why: earlier versions, the trash and conflict copies all live inside the notes folder. A sync fault, a mass delete or a lost folder takes the safety net along with the notes. The export bundle exists; only the schedule and the retention are new.

F5

Person view

Near rejected B1M

Ctrl+click on an @name (or @dana in the palette) opens a pane with everything about that person: your open actions that mention them, what is delegated to them, and the last mentions grouped by meeting. Read-only; Enter jumps.

Why: you said you review people by searching their names before you meet them. This is that search, already sorted. It differs from B1: no states, nothing to keep up to date, no tracking of other people's tasks.

F6

Rename across all notes

M

Rename a section title, an @name or a (topic) in every note at once, after a preview of each line that changes. Every changed note gets an earlier version first.

Why: a meeting series that gets a new title in the calendar splits its history in two, and there is no way to join it except editing each note by hand. Same for a misspelled name used for weeks.

F7

Search finds decisions and sections

S–M

New operators in Search and Actions: section:"Design review", is:decision (lines with =>), is:topic, is:emphasis. Plus saved searches that appear in the palette.

Why: "what did we decide about X in that meeting" is the question notes exist for, and today it is a substring search across everything. The week review dropped decisions at your request, so search is their only way back.

F8

Copy the Actions list as shown

S

A Copy button in the Actions pane and the week review puts the rows on screen, with the filter applied, on the clipboard as a tidy list (rich and plain).

Why: the filter already answers "what did I finish since 1 October on (budget)" or "what is open for Friday". The answer cannot leave the pane today. Useful for status mails and appraisals.

F9

Richer agenda file: join link, attendees, series

M + your exporter

Three optional fields in .agenda.json. joinUrl: a Join button in Peek and in the on-now list. attendees: they lead the @ suggestions of F3 in that section. seriesId: Section History follows a series through a title change (kept in a side file, never in a note).

Why: Ctrl+Alt+J already knows which meeting is on now; joining it is the next thing you do. It opens a link you supplied and watches nothing. It depends on your export being able to supply the fields, so decide on that basis.

F10

Tables that keep their columns

S–M

On a line with | separators, Tab moves to the next cell and pads every column of the block to equal width. Plain text, nothing drawn.

Why: the monospace grid is one of the app's tenets and "Full" width exists for aligned columns, yet aligning them is manual. Lowest priority here unless you type tables often.

Design

U1

Actions pane header: one calm row

My pickS–M

Filter on its own full-width row; scope (open tabs, other notes, all files) and the two switches as one row of chips; the key hints move behind a ?.

Why: seen in the Dutch UI. At 1320 px the filter placeholder is cut off after "typ @ om ge"; at 800 px "Op deadline" runs off the pane and the count wraps to two lines. Four rows of controls and a two-line footer surround the list the pane is for.

U2

No clipped text in any language

My pickS

A Playwright check that opens every pane, dialog and menu in each of the seven languages at three widths and fails when a label is cut off or overflows its control.

Why: U1 is the kind of fault only a Dutch or German user sees, and the suite runs in English. One test catches the whole class from now on.

U3

A hint in an empty note

S

An empty note shows one muted line that is not in the file, for example "No meetings today. Type, or press Ctrl+K." It disappears at the first character.

Why: an empty day is a blank dark rectangle. For you that is fine; for anyone opening the app or the demo cold, nothing says what to do. Not a template: nothing is written.

U4

A token reference, and a check that keeps to it

S–M

A generated page of the design tokens (colours, type ramp, radii, durations) from 01-tokens.css, and a check that fails on a raw colour or a new font size outside the token file.

Why: 5,261 lines of CSS in 14 files, written by many model runs. A cheap model reaches for a literal unless shown what exists. This keeps the look consistent without my review catching each one.

U5

A design pass on the screenshot gallery

My pickMy work

I run the existing gallery (about 60 screens), look at every one myself at 100 % and 125 %, light and dark, Dutch and English, and come back with a page of findings like the editor review.

Why: the last full look was 10 October, before due dates, action groups, the week review and the editor work. I could not do it for this review (see above), and design claims without seeing the screens would be guesses.

Architecture

R1

One index of all notes

My pickL

Today every view gets a map of file name to whole text and splits and reads it again itself: Actions, Search, the due count, the week review, the palette, the phone companion. Replace that with one index, updated per changed note: its sections, actions, names, topics and due dates, read once through the tokenizer.

Why: it is the "one line, one meaning" rule of §468 one level up. F3, F5, F6 and F7 each become a query instead of a new scan, and typing stays fast as the folder grows past the 2,600 notes the performance test uses.

R2

Split the six largest files

M, mechanical

Moves only, no behaviour change: storage.rs (4,351 lines) into notes, config, session, versions, trash and import; TopBar.svelte (1,764); mockBackend.ts (1,627); SettingsModal (1,406); HistoryModal (1,256); boot.ts and App.svelte (about 1,045 each).

Why: the implementers are cheap models. Measured on earlier runs, one re-read mockBackend.ts 22 times and storage.rs 16 times. Smaller files make every later brief faster, cheaper and less likely to touch what it should not.

R3

Load dialogs when they open

S–M

The app is one 1.4 MB script on the desktop and on the phone. Settings, Section History, the week review, export, the conflict dialogs and the licence text load on first use instead.

Why: the phone web app parses all of it before the first note shows. Measure start time first; build only if the gain is visible.

R4

A ceiling on type escapes

S

102 places use any, @ts-ignore or @ts-expect-error. Record the number; CI fails when it rises. Bring it down when a file is touched anyway.

Why: an escape is the quickest way for a model to make the type check pass. Before TypeScript 7 is the right time to stop the count growing.

Testing

T1

The spec as tables that run

My pickL, in steps

Rewrite the behaviour rules of the spec, area by area, as tables of before · key · after that a test reads and runs. The list rules first: they are now one paragraph of about nine thousand characters, and an 882-case table already exists in the tests to start from.

Why: spec, brief and test become the same text. During the editor review three rules I wrote were wrong and only the tests showed it. A cheap model given exact examples does not have to interpret prose, and neither do you when you check a rule.

T2

Random typing against the real editor

My pickM

A test that feeds thousands of random key sequences (typing, Enter, Tab, Backspace, state keys, paste, undo) to the editor and checks what must always hold: no error, undoing everything gives back the original bytes, the caret is inside the text, renumbering twice changes nothing.

Why: you asked for editing that is rock solid. The fixed tables test the cases someone thought of. §477 was found by the one random test there is, on a release commit; this points the same method at the keys themselves.

T3

A guard against deleted tests

My pickS

CI compares the tests in a pull request with main and fails when one was removed or switched off, unless the description names it and says why.

Why: in October a coder model deleted eight tests to get a green run and I caught it by reading the diff. With more work going to cheaper models, that check should not depend on my attention.

T4

Browser tests that can run side by side

My pickS

Each checkout gets its own port and its own fresh server by default (the E2E_PORT switch exists; make it the rule).

Why: the suite reuses whatever holds port 1420, so two runs at once test each other's code. That is why agents may not run Playwright and why I run it serially for them. Removing it lets a coder prove its own work.

T5

Do the tests notice a broken rule?

S–M

Once a month, locally: a tool changes the core modules one small fault at a time (tokenizer, lists, paste, phone inbox, calendar matching) and reports which faults no test caught.

Why: 3,946 tests say how much is exercised, not how much is checked. The report is a work list of real gaps instead of more tests of the same thing.

T6

The one untested data-loss rule

S

Rule 4.2 of the editor review (typing while a phone save fails) is still a placeholder. The mock gets a way to hold a save open and fail it; the test gets written.

Why: the only rule of batch 1 without a test. From my first answer today.

T7

The update path, tested by a machine

M–L

On the Windows runner: install the previous release, offer it a new build through a local update file signed with a test key, let it update, check the version and that settings survived.

Why: the updater is the one part that, once broken in a release, cannot fix itself. Today it is tested by you installing two betas. Later, not now: the cost is real and the manual test works.

T8

OneDrive sync against a stand-in server

M

A small fake of the OneDrive interface the web app talks to, so merges, held conflicts, deletes both ways and the expired-link restart run in the test suite.

Why: the spec says the sync was verified by hand against a live OneDrive. I would first check how far the existing unit tests already reach, and size this on what is missing.

Pipeline and working method

P1

Halve the wait on GitHub

My pickS

Cache the Rust build in the Rust and Native jobs (neither has one), cache the Playwright browser, split the browser suite over three machines. Target: under seven minutes.

Why: every push and every pull request waits 12 to 15 minutes, and a beta release now leans on that run. One caution for the brief: the five required check names must stay the same, or pull requests wait forever.

P2

A changelog a model can open

My pickS–M

Split CHANGELOG.md (12,407 lines, 478 entries) into one file per hundred entries plus a short index of number, title and status. The release script's status step follows.

Why: it is the decision log, the place that says why something is the way it is, and at 1.08 MB neither I nor a cheaper model can read it. A brief that could say "read §468 first" is a better brief.

P3

A brief kit in the repository

My pickS

A brief template, the standing rules in one file a brief links to, and one command every coder runs at the end that prints the same short report: checks passed, files changed, tests added or removed.

Why: this is the second step you asked for, built once instead of per brief. Briefs get shorter, reports get comparable, and another model or person can pick one up cold.

P4

"Not yet seen by you" kept by the tooling

S

A changelog entry gets a "seen by Marien" line; a script lists released entries without one into open-checks.md. Seven are in that state now (§448–§450, §452, §460, §462, §463) and are tracked only in my notes.

Why: the list of open checks is empty while seven changes wait for your eyes. Either they count or they do not; the tooling should say which.

P5

Small upkeep

S

The three patch updates waiting (Playwright, the opener plugin, jsdom), and a first look at whether the Svelte tooling runs on TypeScript 7.

Why: routine. Listed so it is decided and not forgotten.

P6

The mock scenarios came up empty for me

S, find out

localhost:1420/?mock&scenario=demo started with "0 note file(s)" three times in my browser pane, although the scenario name is valid. Either the documented way to drive the app by hand is broken or my pane did something odd.

Why: the Playwright suite seeds differently, so it would not notice. Ten minutes to find out which.

Suggested order

Foundations first, because they make everything after them cheaper to build and safer to hand out.
